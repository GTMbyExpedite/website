# Landing Page Copy

A skill for writing and rewriting landing page copy that is specific, outcome-oriented, and structured for how people actually read marketing pages — scanning headings, reading the first sentence of each section, then deciding whether to go deeper.

## Before writing

### Intake questions

Ask only what the task needs. Skip anything the user's instruction already answers.

1. **Who is this for?** "Who lands on this page, and what are they trying to decide?" The answer shapes vocabulary, specificity level, and what counts as proof. A founder evaluating agencies reads differently from an IC evaluating tools. Skip if the audience is obvious from context or the page already exists and the audience is clear.

2. **What's the one job of this section?** "After reading this section, what should the visitor think, feel, or do?" Every section has one job. A hero makes them stay. A value prop makes them believe. A proof section makes them trust. A CTA makes them act. If the user says "just make it better," ask what "better" means for this specific section.

3. **Any reference?** "Is there a page or piece of copy you like the feel of?" Ask once. If no, look at the existing page for tone and move on.

### Read the page first

Before rewriting any section, read the full page (or at least the sections before and after). Copy that works in isolation can fail in context:
- A punchy hero followed by a punchy value prop followed by a punchy proof section creates slogan fatigue
- A section that repeats what the previous section already said wastes the reader's attention
- A tone shift between sections breaks the voice

## Section-by-section guidance

### Hero

The hero has 2-3 seconds to answer: "Am I in the right place, and is this worth reading?"

- **Headline**: one concrete claim or outcome. Not a category label ("B2B Marketing Solutions"), not a mission statement ("We believe in..."), not a question ("Ready to grow?"). A statement the reader can agree or disagree with.
- **Subheadline**: 1-2 sentences unpacking how. The headline says what, the subheadline says how or for whom.
- **CTA**: one primary action. Label it with what happens when they click ("Book a call", "See the demo"), not with what you want them to do ("Get started", "Learn more").

Avoid: stacking multiple headlines, adding a paragraph of context before the heading, or leading with credentials instead of the reader's problem.

### Value propositions / features

Each block answers: "What do I get, and why does it matter to someone like me?"

- **Lead with the outcome, follow with the mechanism.** "Pipeline that compounds quarterly" (outcome) then explain how the method creates that (mechanism). Not the other way around.
- **One idea per block.** If a feature block tries to cover two things, split it or pick the stronger one.
- **Specific details over adjective stacking.** "60-90 day engagement" beats "rapid, efficient, streamlined process." Numbers, timeframes, named deliverables.

### Social proof / testimonials

- **Lead with the result.** If the testimonial mentions a specific outcome, pull that into the display quote. "Pipeline went from zero to 40 qualified opportunities in one quarter" is stronger than "Great team, really enjoyed working with them."
- **Name and context.** Every testimonial needs a name, role, and company (or at minimum, role and company type). Unattributed quotes read as fabricated.
- **Placement matters.** Proof works best immediately after the claim it supports. A testimonial about pipeline results belongs near the methodology section, not at the bottom of the page.

### CTA / closing section

- **Restate the value, don't summarise the page.** The reader has already scanned the page. Don't recap every section. Instead, restate the single strongest reason to act.
- **Lower the barrier.** "Book a 20-minute call" is easier to say yes to than "Get started." Name what happens next and how long it takes.
- **One CTA.** Not three options. If there are multiple entry points (call, email, form), pick the primary one for the CTA button and put the others as text links.

## Writing rules

### Density management

The biggest problem on iteratively-edited landing pages is slogan density — too many compressed, punchy lines stacked together. Each line sounds clever in isolation, but together they create a wall of performed confidence that feels like a LinkedIn carousel, not a real business.

**The rule: max two punchy lines in proximity.** After a compressed, balanced, or pithy line, the next sentence should be plain explanation — longer, specific, and grounded. This alternating rhythm (punch, explain, punch, explain) reads as confident without being exhausting.

**How to spot density problems:**
- Three or more sentences in a row that are under 10 words each
- Balanced constructions stacked: "Not X. Y." followed by "Don't Z. W."
- Every sentence in a section reads like a pull quote

**How to fix:**
- Keep the two strongest lines. Cut the weakest.
- Replace cut lines with plain sentences that add specifics: a number, a timeframe, a named deliverable, a mechanism.
- Read the section aloud. If it sounds like a montage voiceover, it's too dense.

### Voice consistency

- **Match the page's existing voice.** Don't introduce a different register in one section. If the page speaks directly ("We build your pipeline"), don't switch to passive ("Pipelines are built through our methodology") in one section.
- **Write at the reader's level.** B2B founders don't need jargon explained. IC marketers don't need to be told what ABM is. Match the vocabulary to the audience without dumbing down or showing off.
- **Active voice by default.** "We audit your GTM" not "A comprehensive GTM audit is conducted." Passive voice is fine when the actor genuinely doesn't matter.

### Specificity ladder

Weak copy lives at the top of the abstraction ladder. Strong copy lives closer to the ground.

```
Abstract:   "We drive results"
General:    "We build pipeline"
Specific:   "We build pipeline into your first 100 target accounts"
Concrete:   "60-90 day engagement. Named account list. Your team trained to run it after."
```

Every sentence should be at least at the "Specific" level. The best sentences reach "Concrete." If you catch yourself writing at the "Abstract" or "General" level, push it down one or two rungs by adding a number, a name, a timeframe, or a mechanism.

## Verification

After writing or rewriting copy, check:

1. **Slogan density**: are there more than two punchy lines in a row anywhere?
2. **Outcome test**: does every bullet and description tell the reader what they get?
3. **Repetition**: does any section repeat a point already made elsewhere on the page?
4. **Voice consistency**: does the tone match the rest of the page?
5. **Specificity**: is every sentence at least at the "Specific" level on the ladder?
6. **One job per section**: does each section do exactly one thing?

Fix anything that fails before committing.
